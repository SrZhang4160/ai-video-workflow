Why read a hundred traces before you write any evals?
A trace is the full record of one request:
what the customer said, which tools it called, how it replied.
Many people start by listing everything they want to test,
like a polite tone or a helpful answer.
The problem is, those are only guesses about where it fails.
But the mistakes it really makes are often ones you didn't expect.
Say you read traces and find customers giving two order numbers,
and it checks only the first, then refunds the second too.
You'd never come up with that one sitting at your desk.
Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar teach a course on AI evals.
Their advice is to write evals for errors you discover,
not for errors you imagine.
Write evals without reading traces, and they test your guesses.
A high score then says little about what real customers hit.
So why a hundred?
They suggest at least a hundred, and to keep reading
until new traces stop turning up new kinds of failure.
A hundred is the floor. If you're still learning, keep reading.
As you read, note what went wrong in each one.
If a trace has several problems, note the first one.
That's because later errors are often caused by earlier ones.
Say it looked up the wrong order. The refund amount will be wrong too.
Then group similar notes into a few categories
and count how often each one shows up.
Only then do you know which failure to write evals for first.
And reading traces isn't only for before you write evals.
Anthropic's guide to agent evals makes the same point.
After the evals run, read the transcripts and the grades.
Only then can you tell if the agent really made a mistake,
or the grader rejected a correct answer.
They once found a public benchmark with grading so rigid
that an answer with fewer decimal places was marked wrong.
Without reading traces, you'd blame the agent.
Reading traces also catches bugs in the eval itself.
So writing evals starts with reading traces.
Read at least a hundred, find out where it really fails,
and then write evals for those real failures.
