We're still on grading, now the biases of L L M judges.
Last time, we checked judges against human scores.
But even a good judge has fixed leanings.
They don't track quality, yet they move scores.
Research has measured three well-known ones.
Take our refund agent: a customer's headphones broke.
The judge compares two versions of the reply.
Position bias: it leans to one slot, often the first.
Swap the order, and the verdict can flip.
So when A wins, was it better, or just better placed?
Then verbosity bias: it often favors the longer reply.
One reply explains the refund in three sentences.
The other says the same in three polite paragraphs.
The judge tends to score the long one higher anyway.
Last, self-preference: it favors its own model family.
If the judge and the agent come from the same family,
it may score the agent too high.
And it can't fairly compare its own family with another.
How do we reduce these? Start with position.
Use pairwise comparison: two replies at a time.
It picks the better one, and we run both orders.
Once with A first, once with B first.
Now each reply gets the first slot once.
A only wins if the judge picks it both times.
If the two verdicts differ, the order made the call.
Count it as a tie, or leave it out.
What's left are verdicts that don't change with order.
For verbosity, the fix goes into the rubric.
The rubric says the same information gets no points for length.
You can also add that padding and repetition lose points.
So the judge checks completeness, not word count.
For self-preference, use a judge from another family.
To compare two families, let a third family judge.
Then spot-check some verdicts against human scores.
So an L L M judge isn't neutral.
It leans to a slot, to longer replies, and to its own family.
The core: swap the order, fix the rubric, switch the judge.
