This episode: how to group a pile of failures, and what to fix first.
Say you built a customer-service agent for refunds.
It looks up orders, issues refunds, and emails customers.
You read a hundred of its recent conversation logs,
and find plenty went wrong, in all sorts of ways.
If you fix each problem the moment you see it,
you keep patching small things and never reach the big ones.
So group them first, then rank them, and only then fix.
The first step is called open coding.
You go through the logs one by one, taking notes.
The notes don't follow any categories set in advance.
Whatever goes wrong, you write it in plain words.
For example, "didn't check the order was really theirs,"
or "the email gave the wrong refund amount."
Why not set the categories first and sort into them?
Because then you only look for mistakes you expected,
and the new ones it really makes slip past you.
If one conversation goes wrong in several places,
note only the first place it went wrong,
because later mistakes often follow from that one.
With the notes done, step two is axial coding.
You put notes about the same problem into one group.
Take "didn't check who owns the order"
and "no order number." Both mean "didn't verify the buyer."
After grouping, you're left with just a few failure types.
Then you count how often each type shows up.
So do you fix the most common one first?
Not always. You also ask how bad each failure is.
Suppose a cold, stiff tone shows up ten times,
and an unauthorized refund only once.
A stiff tone at worst leaves a customer a bit unhappy.
But a refund over five hundred dollars paid without approval
means real money is gone, and hard to get back.
So rank by frequency and severity together,
and that one unauthorized refund goes to the top.
Some mistakes have an obvious fix, so just fix them.
The ones that keep coming back each get a judge.
Each judge checks one type and says pass or fail.
That keeps its verdicts clear and easy to check.
