There are three Rs in strawberry. Many L L Ms famously said two.
The reason: a model never sees letters at all.
Let's build up how an L L M works, and then that will make sense.
At its core, an L L M does one thing: predict the next token.
Give it "The cat sat on the", and it scores every possible next piece.
"mat" gets a high chance, "floor" less, "banana" almost none.
It picks one, appends it, and repeats, token by token.
A token is a chunk of text: a word, part of a word, or punctuation.
For example, "unbelievable" might be un, believ, able: three ids.
APIs bill per token: your input plus the output.
Common words are often one token, and splits differ by tokenizer.
A model might see "strawberry" as, for example, str, aw, berry.
It gets three token ids, never the ten letters inside them.
So a letter count is a guess from patterns, not a real count.
Asking it to spell the word out first, or to use code, usually helps.
Attention is how it decides which words matter. Take this sentence.
To understand "it", that token looks at every other token,
and weighs "dog" most, since roads don't get tired.
Every token does this at once, layer after layer, to predict the next.
First, pretraining: predict the next token on huge amounts of text.
Now it knows language and facts, but it only continues text.
Then S F T, supervised fine-tuning: it learns from example requests with good answers.
Then preference tuning, like R L H F: people compare answers,
and the model is pushed toward the answers people prefer.
The context window is how many tokens it can see at once.
Instructions, history, documents, question and answer all share it.
Whatever does not fit, the model simply cannot see.
So apps commonly trim, summarize, or pick only the relevant parts.
Training rewards text that sounds plausible, not text that is checked.
So ask about an obscure paper, and it may still answer fluently.
It can be wrong, with nothing inside flagging it as wrong.
You can reduce it: sources in the context, "I don't know", checks.
But you can't remove it: generating likely text is what it does.
Reasoning models write out steps before the final answer.
That thinking is output tokens too: you usually pay, and you wait.
It is worth it for math, code and multi-step problems,
and often wasted on a simple lookup.
To read an image, a model first cuts it into small square patches.
Each patch becomes a list of numbers, like a text token does.
Those sit next to your text tokens, and it predicts the answer.
So images take up context space too, and usually cost tokens.
In one picture: text and images become tokens in the context window,
attention mixes them, and it predicts the next token, over and over.
Pretraining, S F T and preference tuning shaped what it predicts.
