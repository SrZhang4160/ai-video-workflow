How many Rs in strawberry? Three. Yet many L L Ms famously said two.
Why? Most people think: it's just bad at spelling. Not quite.
Here's the thing: the model never sees the letters at all.
This is part two of Road to Agent Evals.
So let's open it up, piece by piece. Then strawberry makes sense.
So what does an L L M actually do? One thing: predict the next token.
Give it "The cat sat on the", and it scores every possible next piece.
"mat" gets a high chance, "floor" less, "banana" almost none.
Then it picks one, adds it to the end, and does it all again.
But it doesn't always take the top pick. Sometimes "floor" wins.
So the same prompt can give you different answers, run to run.
That's why evals often run a case several times, not just once.
So what's a token? Most people guess: a word. Close, but not quite.
It's a chunk of text: a word, part of a word, or punctuation.
Take unbelievable. It might be split into un, beleev, able.
Each piece becomes an I D number. Common words are often one token.
And this is also how A P Is bill you: per token, input plus output.
Back to strawberry. It might be split as, say, S T R, A W, berry.
So the model gets three token I Ds. Not the ten letters inside them.
So when you ask it to count the Rs, it can't look. It guesses.
A fix that usually helps: have it spell the word out, or use code.
Next: how does it know which words go together? Attention.
Take this sentence. What does "it" mean here? The dog, or the road?
Here's how: each token can look back at every token before it.
By "tired", the model links "it" to "dog". Roads don't get tired.
This happens in every layer, and it sharpens the next-token guess.
So how does a text predictor become an assistant? Three steps.
Step one, pretraining: predict the next token on huge amounts of text.
Now it knows language and facts. But it only continues text.
Ask it a question, and it might just write more questions.
Step two, S F T, supervised fine-tuning: it learns from example requests with good answers.
Step three, preference tuning, like R L H F: people compare answers,
and the model is pushed toward the answers people prefer.
Now, how much can it read at once? That's the context window.
It's measured in tokens. And everything has to fit inside it:
instructions, history, documents, your question, and the answer.
Whatever doesn't fit, the model simply can't see.
So apps commonly trim old turns, summarize, or pick what's relevant.
Why does it make things up? People say it's lying. It isn't.
Here's why: training rewards text that sounds right, not checked text.
So ask about an obscure paper, and it may still answer fluently.
You can reduce it: sources in the context, "I don't know", checks.
But you can't remove it: generating likely text is what it does.
That's a big reason evals exist: to catch confident wrong answers.
Some models think first: they write out steps, then the final answer.
But that thinking is output tokens too. You usually pay, and you wait.
So is it worth it? For math, code and multi-step problems, yes.
For a simple lookup, like a capital city, it's often wasted.
How does a model "see" an image? It cuts it into small square patches.
Each patch becomes a list of numbers, like a text token does.
Those sit next to your text tokens, and it predicts the answer.
So images take up context space too, and usually cost tokens.
Let's put it together. Text and images become tokens in the window,
attention mixes them, and it predicts the next token, over and over.
Pretraining, S F T and preference tuning shaped what it predicts.
And three traps: letters hide in tokens, plausible isn't true,
and thinking costs extra tokens. Save this picture for later.
