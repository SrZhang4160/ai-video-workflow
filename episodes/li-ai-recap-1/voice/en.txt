Ask people what an AI engineer does, and most will say: train models.
And training a big model is a huge job. GPUs, data, months of work.
But here's the thing: most AI engineers never train one.
They take a model someone else trained, and call it with a request.
Then they build the whole product around that call.
So let's unpack it: the job, the stack, and one API call.
So where do AI engineers fit? Picture three kinds of engineers.
One trains the model itself. That's the ML or research engineer.
Another builds servers, databases and APIs. That's backend.
And the AI engineer? They build the product on top of both.
They call the model all day long. They rarely train it.
Let's make it concrete. Say you're building a support assistant.
A customer asks: "Where is my order?" So what do you actually build?
First, a prompt: instructions that tell the model how to behave.
Then retrieval, so it answers from your real help articles.
Then tools, like a function that actually looks up the order.
Then evals, so next week's change doesn't quietly break it.
And then you run it for real: cost, speed, errors, safety.
Notice how little of that is the model. It's mostly software.
Now stack those pieces up. The model sits at the bottom.
Right above it: the prompt and context you send it.
Retrieval and tools feed that context, from your data and your code.
Evals sit off to the side, and test the whole thing.
And production wraps around all of it: speed, cost, monitoring.
Why so many layers? Because the model only knows what you send it.
It's never seen your company's docs, or the orders placed today.
If it's not in the request, the model simply can't see it.
So retrieval and tools fetch those facts, and put them in. Every call.
Another catch: ask the same question twice, and the wording changes.
So you can't just check for one exact string, like a normal test.
Instead you build evals: real questions, and a check for each answer.
Change the prompt, run the evals, and you see what broke.
Think of evals as tests for answers that never come out the same.
OK, let's zoom all the way in, to a single API call.
Everything we just talked about is built on this one request.
What's in it? Instructions, the chat so far, and the new question.
Plus a few settings, like which model, and how long the reply can be.
First thing that happens: your text gets chopped into tokens.
A token is a word, or a piece of one. Here, each word is one token.
Each token turns into a number. Numbers are all the model ever sees.
Rule of thumb in English: a token is about three quarters of a word.
Then the model does one thing: it guesses the next token. Just one.
That token gets added on, and it guesses again. And again.
Until it outputs a special 'end' token, or hits your length limit.
That's why long answers take longer. Every token is another step.
With streaming, you see the words show up as they're made.
Without it, you wait, and then get the whole reply at once.
And every response tells you how many tokens went in, and came out.
Why care? Because you pay for both, often at different rates.
So next time someone asks you what an AI engineer does, here you go.
They build products on top of a model. They mostly don't train it.
They wire up the stack: context, retrieval, tools, evals, production.
And under all of it is one call: tokens in, tokens out, paid for both.
