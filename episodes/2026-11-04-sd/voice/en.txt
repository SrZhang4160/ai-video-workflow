Ten million people. Is one server enough?
The tech director said yes.
Xiaoman, an intern on day three, said no.
With simple arithmetic, she proved him wrong in front of everyone.
It started last night.
C E O Wang posted in the team chat.
Tomorrow at eight PM, a top streamer sells our products live.
Ten million people expected to watch!
Tech director Zhou Kai replied in a second:
Relax. One server can handle it.
The chat filled with thumbs-ups.
Only Xiaoman frowned.
She had one question:
Ten million people. How many requests per second?
That number is Q P S: queries per second.
To estimate it, you need three numbers:
how many people come,
how many requests each person sends,
and how many seconds those requests squeeze into.
Next morning, Zhou Kai scoffed:
An intern talking architecture?
Ten million people, ten requests each: a hundred million requests.
Divide by the eighty-six thousand four hundred seconds in a day.
Just over a thousand Q P S.
One server is more than enough.
Xiaoman stood up.
Mr. Zhou, your math is wrong.
Live sales don't spread people over a whole day.
At eight PM sharp, everyone rushes in at once.
Averages lie.
Xiaoman walked to the whiteboard.
Say ten million people arrive in the first ten minutes,
each viewing, refreshing, ordering: about ten requests.
Ten million times ten is a hundred million requests.
Ten minutes is six hundred seconds.
A hundred million divided by six hundred: about one hundred seventy thousand Q P S.
And that's still a ten-minute average. The busiest second is higher.
One hundred seventy thousand, versus about one thousand one hundred.
Off by more than a hundred times.
All because he used the whole-day average.
The room went silent.
So how many servers?
Say load tests show one server handles two thousand Q P S.
One hundred seventy thousand divided by two thousand: eighty-five servers.
Zhou Kai's plan had one.
And per-server capacity comes from load tests, not gut feeling.
Zhou Kai went pale: those numbers are all guesses!
Xiaoman: yes. Estimating means putting assumptions on the table.
A wrong assumption can be fixed.
Not doing the math is gambling.
Xiaoman's three steps. Save this:
One: total requests equals people times requests each.
Two: divide by the peak window, not the whole day.
Three: divide by one server's Q P S, then add headroom.
A common practice: thirty to fifty percent extra.
Wang decided: "We go with Xiaoman. Add servers tonight!"
Eighty-five servers, all in place.
At eight PM the stream starts. The servers hold.
But three minutes in,
every monitor turned red.
This time, it wasn't the servers that broke...
Next: "The Disk Is Full."
Eighty-five servers were fine.
So why did the database fall first?
Xiaoman has a second number to estimate: storage.
Quick quiz: a million people arrive within one minute,
six requests each. Roughly what is the peak Q P S?
A, ten thousand, or B, a hundred thousand? Tell me in the comments.
