Let's walk the whole pipeline, and see where to look when it fails.
Ahead of time, you split your documents into chunks,
turn each chunk into a vector, and store it.
Then, for every question, you turn the question into a vector too,
search for the nearest chunks, and rerank them,
and put the best ones in the prompt, so the model can answer.
An embedding model turns text into a vector: a list of numbers.
Picture each vector as a point on a map.
Similar meaning lands close together, even with no shared words.
Unrelated text lands far away.
To compare two texts, you compare their vectors.
Cosine similarity looks at the angle between them, not the length.
Pointing the same way: close to one. Very similar.
At a right angle: close to zero. Unrelated.
Retrieval then means: find the chunks with the highest score.
A model has never seen your handbook, or last week's changes.
RAG fixes that: first, retrieve the most relevant chunks,
then paste them into the prompt, next to the question.
The model answers from those chunks, and can cite them.
So the answer can only be as good as what you retrieved.
Before you embed, you split documents into chunks.
Too big, and one chunk mixes many topics: a noisy match.
Too small, and a chunk loses its context.
A common middle: one idea per chunk, with a little overlap.
There's no universal best size: test a few with your evals.
A vector database stores each vector with its text and metadata.
Checking a question against every vector gets slow at scale,
so it uses approximate nearest-neighbour search: close matches, fast.
Metadata filters narrow the search, like: only the 2026 handbook.
Examples: P G vector inside Postgres, Qdrant, Weaviate, Pinecone.
Vector search matches meaning. But some words must match exactly:
error codes, product names, I Ds, like E four zero one two.
To an embedding, E four zero one two and E four zero two one can look almost the same.
Keyword search, like B M twenty-five, rewards exact and rare terms.
Hybrid search runs both, and merges the two ranked lists.
Search is fast but rough, so fetch many candidates, say fifty.
A reranker reads the question and each chunk together, and scores it.
It is slower but more precise, so it only sees the shortlist.
The top few go into the prompt: less noise, usually better answers.
When RAG answers wrong, first look at the chunks it retrieved.
Right chunk missing? Retrieval problem: fix chunking, search, filters.
Right chunk there? Generation problem: the model didn't use it.
In our example, the old handbook was retrieved. A filter fixed it.
If your knowledge is small and stable, you may not need RAG.
Put all of it in the prompt: nothing to retrieve, nothing to miss.
Same if it's not a lookup: rewriting an email, or text already pasted.
Use RAG when knowledge is large, changes often, or needs sources.
