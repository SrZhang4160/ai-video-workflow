If the judge is wrong, every score is wrong.
A model grading refund emails by a rubric is an L L M judge.
Skimming a few that look right doesn't count,
because you mostly see easy ones and miss the hard ones.
The fix: use people's judgment as the standard and compare.
Pull a batch of replies: good, bad and hard ones,
and have people who know the refund policy use the same rubric
to mark each item yes or no. Those are human labels.
The judge grades the same batch. The share of matching answers is the agreement rate.
Ninety matches out of a hundred is ninety percent.
But compute agreement for each rubric item separately.
Most emails are fine to begin with,
so a judge that passes everything still hits ninety percent.
So check whether it catches the ones people failed.
Then read where they disagree. There are usually three causes.
A vague item: does "refunded soon" count as a time?
Then change it to require the number of days.
The judge lacks facts, like whether approval happened, so give it that.
Or the person was wrong, so fix the label.
After each fix, rerun the judge and compare again.
If an item never lines up, try a stronger model or leave it to people.
Whenever the model or rubric changes, recheck.
Asked in an interview whether a judge is reliable? Say:
Compare it against human labels.
Measure agreement per rubric item.
Recheck whenever the model or rubric changes.
