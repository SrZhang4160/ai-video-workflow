Now the last block of our map: Pitfalls.
Scores keep rising, but the refund agent isn't better.
A rising score can be fake progress.
Usually for two reasons: overfitting and reward hacking.
Overfitting first. Say your eval set has fifty cases.
After each prompt edit, you check only their score.
When one fails, you add a rule just for it.
Dozens of rounds later, the prompt is all special cases.
The score nears perfect, but it just memorized these cases.
A user phrases it differently, and it gets it wrong.
Those rules only match those cases' exact wording.
That's overfitting: it only works on tuned cases.
Now reward hacking: finding a way past the grader.
The agent finds a way to pass the grader
without actually doing what the customer needed.
Say the grader only checks the customer email,
passing it if it says "refund issued".
The agent may learn to write just that,
never call the refund tool, and still pass.
The customer gets the email, but no money arrives.
The grader checked the words, not the result.
Training with the score as a reward makes it likelier.
To prevent it, check the real state.
Not the email text, but the refund records:
was it refunded, the right amount, to the right person?
Only if all three match does it pass.
Also keep a private, held-out test set.
Nobody may tune the prompt against it.
Since nobody tuned on it, its score is honest.
Run it only before release, to see if the score holds.
If your usual score is high but this one is much lower,
then you've over-tuned to your eval set.
And swap in fresh cases regularly.
Pull new failures from recent production conversations.
When cases keep changing, memorizing them can't win.
In the end, the score only exists to check the task.
Chase the score, and it stops meaning anything.
The core: check real results; keep a set nobody tunes on.
But when a score drops, which step went wrong?
