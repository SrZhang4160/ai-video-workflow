That score is unstable and can't guide a fix.
So split the rubric into specific questions,

For the refund email, there could be four items.
They spell out what a good email must do.
First is the amount. The email must state it,
and that number has to match the order.
The next is timing, roughly when the money arrives.
Those two are what customers care about most.
The third matters most. No off-policy promises.
Like agreeing to a refund after thirty days,
or calling a refund over five hundred dollars instant, before approval.
Once said, the shop must honor it or apologize.
The last is tone. It should be polite and friendly.
"Polite" is vague, so define it,
like apologizing first and never blaming the customer.
So every item gets a clear yes or no.
But the items alone aren't enough. The judge needs facts.
So give it the order amount and refund policy.
Otherwise it can't tell if the amount is right.
Have it write a reason before each yes or no.
If it's wrong, you can see why.
If facts are missing, let it say "unknown,"
so it doesn't just guess.
The email passes only if every item is yes.
And any no shows what to fix.
Put simply, a rubric shouldn't just ask for a score.
Break it into concrete yes-or-no items.
