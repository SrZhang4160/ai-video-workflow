Today, let's see why simulated customers are too easy to deal with.
Having a model play the customer is fast and cheap.
But the customer it plays is quite different from a real one.
Let's see where they differ, and how that skews the scores.
The first difference is that it's far too patient.
A Google research team pointed out in a blog post
that simulated users are often unnaturally patient,
and much more polite than real people.
That's because language models are trained
to be helpful, good-natured assistants.
So playing an impatient, difficult customer doesn't come naturally.
Picture this with our refund agent.
The agent asks the same question over and over,
and the simulated customer politely answers every time.
A real customer might have lost patience and left.
So the simulator quietly forgives the agent's flaws,
and the score comes out higher than it really is.
The second problem is which model plays the customer.
A paper at I C L R twenty twenty-six called Lost in Simulation
ran experiments on tau bench's retail tasks.
With the same agent and different models playing the user,
success rates differed by up to nine percentage points.
The paper also compared against real people in four countries,
and found the simulated users misjudged difficulty too.
On hard tasks, they underestimated how well the agent did,
and on medium tasks, they overestimated it.
So part of the score really measures the actor.
So what can we do? There are three main fixes.
The first is to calibrate the simulator against real conversations.
Sample real conversations between customers and the agent,
compare the simulator's patience and when it gives up,
and if they differ a lot, fix its prompt.
Next, keep the model that plays the customer fixed.
Because if you swap the actor and the score moves,
you can't tell whether the agent changed or the actor did.
Finally, add a few difficult customers on purpose,
like one who's vague about the order, or insists after a no.
So by default, simulated customers are too easy, and scores run high.
Calibrate them on real people, fix the actor, add tough roles,
and simulated scores get closer to reality.
