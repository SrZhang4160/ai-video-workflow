This episode: who decides whether an agent did a good job.
Take a customer-service agent that handles refunds.
It looks up orders, issues refunds, and emails customers.
An engineer reads a conversation and thinks it went fine.
The head of support reads it and says that refund was wrong.
With two different standards, you can't grade anything,
because the same conversation gets two opposite scores.
So who should set that standard?
The person who knows the business best, like the head of support.
Because whether a refund is right or wrong
comes from business rules, not from an engineer's guess.
Eval consultant Hamel Husain has a name for this person.
He calls them the "benevolent dictator."
They hear everyone out, but they make the final call.
With one person deciding, the labels don't contradict each other.
He recommends this for most small and mid-sized companies.
Only big teams across many areas need several labelers,
and then you have to measure how often they agree.
Once the expert sets the standard, you write it down clearly.
A product requirement is often just one sentence,
like "refunds should be fast and safe."
You can't grade that, because "safe" is too vague.
So you break it into conditions you can check one by one.
Safe means, first, it looked up the order before refunding,
and confirmed this customer really bought it.
Next, for anything over five hundred dollars, it didn't refund on its own,
but passed it to a human for approval first.
Also, for orders older than thirty days, it declines,
and explains the reason to the customer.
Each of these can be checked in the logs as a yes or a no.
How do you know the conditions are clear enough?
Anthropic's engineering blog gives a simple test.
Have two experts judge the same conversation separately.
For a good task, they should reach the same verdict.
If they don't, the condition is still ambiguous,
and the expert needs to rewrite it more clearly.
The more real conversations experts read, the sharper it gets,
so the list of conditions keeps getting updated.
In the end, the person who knows the business decides,
and the standard becomes conditions you can check.
