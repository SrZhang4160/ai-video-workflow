This episode: how to pull a ground-truth set from production.
A ground-truth set is a batch of real conversations,
each labeled by experts with what doing it right means.
With it, you can check whether automated grading is accurate.
So why take it from production?
Because cases you write cover only what you can imagine,
and real customers ask in far more ways than that.
Shopify's assistant for merchants, Sidekick,
moved away from carefully curated "golden" datasets
toward ground-truth sets that match real production traffic.
So can you just pick a hundred at random?
The trouble is, random picks miss rare but important cases.
Suppose nine in ten refunds are small orders.
In a random hundred, few will be over five hundred dollars.
Yet the big ones that need approval matter most.
So you use a method called stratified sampling.
First split traffic into layers by customer, amount, and problem,
then sample some from each layer.
That way big, late, and someone else's orders all show up.
Keep some purely random picks in each batch too,
so you don't miss a kind you never thought to split out.
Once sampled, the business experts label them.
One labeler alone brings their own leanings.
Shopify's advice is at least three product experts
labeling the same conversations on several criteria.
Then you measure how much they agree,
often with a metric called Cohen's kappa.
It discounts the agreement you'd get by chance.
One means full agreement; zero is about as good as guessing.
Where even experts disagree, the standard isn't clear,
so settle it together before you move on.
With this set, you can test your model judge.
Have it grade the same set, and compare with the experts.
Shopify has shared their own numbers.
At first, their judge's kappa with humans was only zero point zero two,
barely better than random.
After many rounds of work, it reached zero point six one,
against a human baseline of zero point six nine.
Only then was the judge close to human level.
So sample by layers, then have several experts label.
