This episode is about human labeling: people judging pass or fail.
Our refund agent finishes a conversation with a customer,
and someone has to decide whether it got it right.
The verdicts people give are called labels.
Your test set's answer key and your judge model's calibration
both depend on these human labels.
But would a different person give the same labels?
Say a customer bought something thirty-two days ago,
and the agent said it's past thirty days, so no refund.
Wang thinks it followed the rule, so he marks it a pass.
Li thinks it should have offered to pass it to a human,
so she marks it a fail. They disagree.
If only one person labels, you'd never notice
that the standard exists only in that person's head.
So have two or three people label the same batch,
each on their own, without talking it over first.
Then count how many items they labeled the same.
That share is called the agreement rate.
But raw agreement has a flaw: chance can inflate it.
Suppose both people mark almost everything as a pass.
Even without reading closely, they'd mostly match.
Cohen's kappa is designed to correct for that.
It first looks at how often each person marks a pass,
and works out how much they'd agree by chance alone.
Then it measures how far real agreement goes beyond that.
A kappa of one means they agree completely.
A kappa of zero means no better than chance.
When agreement is low, don't blame the labelers first.
Pull out the items they disagreed on and review them together.
Often you'll find the rule itself was never written clearly.
For example, past thirty days, should it offer a human?
The guide didn't say, so everyone had to guess.
So write that case into the labeling guide,
with a clear rule and an example to go with it.
If they still can't agree, a domain expert makes the call,
and the reason gets written into the guide.
Then everyone labels a fresh batch, separately again.
So human labeling means several people labeling separately,
and using kappa to see how much they agree beyond chance.
Wherever they disagree is where the rules need work.
